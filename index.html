Philosophy

Design a feeling, then build the game around it.

Games are meant to make players feel the fantasies that are out of reach. Making a game should always put players' feelings first, whether it's a mighty hero, a god reshaping the world, or a home renovator. When you design a game that taps into players' feelings and fantasies, the experience stays with them.

That approach runs through everything I've made, from co-designing gods, items, and secrets on Final Spark to push the fantasy of controlling gods at your whim, to a horror game that makes you feel like a child playing your first horror game on the Game Boy.

Current project

Final Spark

Design Lead

A 3D roguelike auto-battler where you control gods from across multiple mythologies through secrets and mystery.

My role

Design lead working directly under the game director. I co-designed the game's core systems with the director: gods and classes, essences, Prime Gods, items and relics, and secrets. I also lead a team of around nine designers who build out the content within those systems, from new essences to items and relics.

Gods and classes

Six gods from six pantheons, each with its own class: Anubis (Egyptian, Sage), Oya (Yoruba, Juggernaut), The Morrigan (Celtic, Sorcerer), Ganesha (Hindu, Warden), Ame-no-Uzume (Shinto, Virtuoso) and Itzpapalotl (Aztec, Reaper).

Essences

Assignable passives that reshape a god's playstyle through its auto attacks, active abilities, stats or unique effects. The Dancer, for example, turns auto attacks into a slicing dance that hits every enemy around the unit, while Patient Wellspring trades early tempo for mana over a long fight.

Items and relics

Items are the core source of run-to-run variance, giving units buffs, boons and traits. Relics add passive effects at different tiers of strength and rarity that push the economy, items and unit strength, like R11, which makes every fifth allied auto-attack hit 25% harder.

  • Prime GodsEach Prime God gives your side of the board a unique effect, so where you place units before combat becomes a real decision.
  • SecretsHidden key items and constellations carry the game's mystery, each specified from how it's found to what it looks like and how it plays, such as Gungnir, Odin's spear.

CU-Raiders

Designer and producer

A competitive set-collection game for four players. Curators race across a hidden map of hex tiles around a museum, collecting artifacts from the desert, the tundra and the plains. After eight rounds, whoever holds the most valuable collection becomes the museum's permanent curator.

Design goal

Players will feel excitement and competition when exploring the unknown map collecting artifacts while gaining the satisfaction of set completion.

  • My roleDesigner and producer on a team of four. I focused on making the game's systems work together and on getting the game ready to ship.
  • Hidden mapHoneycomb tiles start face-down around the museum, so every game's map is shuffled and revealed as players explore.
  • ExplorationPlayers move two hexes a turn and draw from that region's deck. Cards can be artifacts, events or junk, and a tile goes dead once it has been left.
  • Set collectionArtifacts score 3 to 10 points alone, while sets such as the Trinity of Japan or the Set of Death score up to 45. With a four-artifact hand limit, every keep is a choice.
  • TradingPlayers can trade freely on their turn without spending it, so deals and rivalries shape who completes which set.

Game Proposal: Dragons & Defendants

A pitch for a third-person detective and lawyer role-playing game that combines the essence of Ace Attorney, the detective gameplay of Disco Elysium, and the rules and laws of fantasy worlds. You play a first-year associate at a fantasy law firm, handed three major cases that could make you a partner.

The hook

“Don't invite vampires in.” “Don't eat fae food.” Everyone knows the rules from fairy tales. Here they are the law.

  • Core loopEach case runs through three segments: a 3D detective section to explore the scene and collect evidence, an interview and case-building section, then an Ace Attorney-style courtroom showdown.
  • Fantasy lawInvestigations run on the practices and laws of witches, and on witnesses who give their clues as riddles. Veterans get new rules to navigate, and new players get a more whimsical world.
  • Visual direction2D visual-novel characters, isometric 3D investigation scenes, and a contemporary fantasy city where phones and cars exist alongside magic.
  • ScopeThree full cases of about 20 minutes each, three short cutscenes, three modeled crime locations, one rigged 3D protagonist, 7 to 10 2D characters, and partial voice acting.
  • Production planA 12-month timeline from pre-production to a gold master shown at expo, for a team of 22 plus the director, built in Unity with Photoshop, Blender and Maya for art.

Pavlova

A Souls-like, combat-focused game about what it means to be human. You play as Anya, a ballerina fighting through a post-apocalyptic Victorian London plagued by the sounds of a cosmic horror, with combat built on music and rhythm.

Pitch quote

“The only reason for mastering technique is to make sure the body does not prevent the soul from expressing itself.” — La Meri

  • Rhythm combatAttacks landed on the beat hit harder. Players string dance combos together with the right timing, and some combos don't work on some enemies, so they have to adapt their dance to what works.
  • Music and augmentsLeg augments act like weapons, with one equipped at a time. Music is what makes or breaks a fight: each enemy type has to be matched with the right music and moves.
  • Core loopEnter an area, explore and uncover what happened there, optionally help locals, and fight through smaller enemies to the area's boss.
  • Helping LondonHelping residents earns boons such as items, shortcuts, secret areas and help with bosses, and in rare cases changes the story's outcome.
  • Hard choicesResidents force moral dilemmas, like killing someone to make your life easier or saving them and making it harder. These choices decide the ending.
  • Death as learningEach fight ends one way: you win or you die. As in any Souls-like, dying is part of learning, and you return to the last save point you used.

Combat UIThe selected music, combo counter and beat indicator sit at the edges, so the dancer and the enemy stay at the centre of the screen.

The story

In an alternate Victorian era, humans drew magic from sounds coming from space, until it twisted minds and bodies into an affliction called the Aria. Queen Victoria sealed the afflicted away in a lost catacomb, the Concert Hall, but the tune was made by eldritch horrors seeking a new civilisation to take hold of. Decades later, Anya, a “Deafened” recruit immune to the Aria, is sent into an overrun London to find the source.

Game flow
  1. St Paul's
  2. Big Ben
  3. Westminster Abbey
  4. Buckingham Palace
  5. Tower of London
  6. Tower Bridge
  7. The Concert Hall

Big Ben and Westminster Abbey can be taken in either order. The final bosses open only after visiting Queen Victoria.

  • ThemeDark gothic Victorian, built to bring a steady sense of dread.
  • DecisionsMost choices don't change the overall story, but every choice changes the world.
  • ChallengeMastering the combat. It's most of the game, so it should never feel like a nuisance.
  • EmotionConstant unease. Not a horror game, but tension builds and releases like one.
